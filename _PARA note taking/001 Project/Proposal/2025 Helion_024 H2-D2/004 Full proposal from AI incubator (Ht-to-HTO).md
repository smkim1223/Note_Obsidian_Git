## 1. Executive Summary

The efficient conversion of hydrogen-tritium gas mixtures to tritiated water (HT → HTO) represents a critical technological challenge in fusion fuel cycle management, particularly for advanced reactor concepts pursued by Helion Energy and the broader fusion community. Current approaches rely heavily on noble metal catalysts (Pt, Pd) that suffer from prohibitive costs, limited availability, radiological degradation, and cycling instability under the demanding conditions of fusion fuel processing systems.

This proposal outlines a systematic research program to develop and validate low-cost, non-noble metal catalyst systems capable of facilitating hydrogen isotope exchange reactions at near-ambient temperatures while maintaining superior durability and industrial scalability. Our approach leverages Pacific Northwest National Laboratory's (PNNL) Integrated Institute for Catalysis and foundational expertise in the DOE Basic Energy Sciences (BES) Catalysis Program to design novel bimetallic and supported catalyst platforms based on earth-abundant metals including Ni, Cu, Co, and Fe in optimized configurations.

The research strategy progresses systematically from fundamental mechanistic understanding through surrogate testing using hydrogen-deuterium exchange (HD → D₂O) to full tritium-compatible validation (HT → HTO). This staged approach ensures safe development while maintaining direct relevance to Helion Energy's operational requirements and DOE's national fusion energy mission. Key innovations include: (1) mechanistically-guided design of non-noble bimetallic catalysts with tunable electronic properties, (2) advanced support engineering to enhance low-temperature activity and poison resistance, (3) operando spectroscopic characterization to elucidate structure-function relationships, and (4) integrated durability testing under realistic cycling conditions.

The proposed work advances the Technology Readiness Level (TRL) from fundamental discovery (TRL 1-2) through laboratory validation (TRL 3-5) to system demonstration and deployment readiness (TRL 6-7). PNNL's unique combination of world-class catalysis research capabilities, radiological handling infrastructure, and deep mechanistic expertise in metal-hydrogen interactions positions this team to deliver transformative catalyst technologies that directly support Helion Energy's commercial fusion development while advancing DOE's scientific understanding of isotopic catalysis phenomena. Expected outcomes include 10-fold cost reductions relative to noble metal systems, enhanced durability under fusion-relevant conditions, and clear pathways to industrial manufacturing and deployment.

## 2. Background and Purpose

### Technical Motivation for Hydrogen Isotope Exchange in Fusion Systems

Fusion energy systems, particularly those being developed by Helion Energy, require sophisticated fuel cycle management to handle hydrogen isotopes including protium (H), deuterium (D), and tritium (T). The conversion of gaseous hydrogen-tritium mixtures to tritiated water (HT → HTO) is essential for several critical operations: tritium inventory management, contamination control, waste processing, and fuel purification. This isotope exchange reaction enables the safe capture and concentration of tritium from gas streams while facilitating its subsequent recovery and reuse in the fusion process.

Current state-of-the-art systems rely predominantly on precious metal catalysts, particularly platinum and palladium supported on various substrates. While these materials demonstrate acceptable catalytic activity, they present fundamental limitations that impede commercial deployment and long-term operational sustainability in fusion environments.

### Limitations of Current Noble Metal Catalysts

Noble metal catalysts face four primary challenges that motivate the development of alternative systems. First, economic considerations are paramount: platinum and palladium costs have increased dramatically, with current prices exceeding $1,000-$2,000 per troy ounce. For industrial-scale fusion fuel processing, catalyst costs can represent 20-30% of total system capital expenditure, creating unsustainable economic barriers to deployment.

Second, supply chain vulnerabilities pose significant risks to fusion energy commercialization. Global platinum and palladium production is concentrated in geopolitically sensitive regions, with limited reserves relative to projected fusion industry demand. This concentration creates strategic supply risks that could constrain fusion energy deployment if not addressed through alternative catalyst technologies.

Third, radiological degradation represents a fundamental technical limitation. Extended exposure to tritium beta radiation and neutron flux in fusion environments causes structural changes in noble metal catalyst systems, including sintering, phase segregation, and support degradation. These effects lead to progressive activity loss and necessitate frequent catalyst replacement, increasing operational costs and generating radioactive waste streams.

Fourth, cycling stability under dynamic operational conditions remains problematic. Fusion fuel processing systems undergo frequent startup/shutdown cycles and variable temperature excursions that stress catalyst materials through thermal cycling, oxidation-reduction transitions, and mechanical strain. Noble metal catalysts demonstrate limited tolerance for such cycling, with measurable performance degradation occurring within 100-500 cycles under realistic conditions.

### Need for Low-Temperature, Robust Catalyst Systems

Helion Energy's fusion reactor design philosophy emphasizes compact, efficient systems operating under moderate thermal conditions to maximize energy conversion efficiency and minimize infrastructure complexity. This approach creates specific requirements for hydrogen isotope exchange catalysts: operation at near-room temperature (20-60°C), rapid kinetics to minimize processing time and equipment size, exceptional durability under cycling conditions, and compatibility with existing process engineering systems.

Low-temperature operation is particularly challenging for isotope exchange reactions, as conventional catalysts typically require elevated temperatures (>150°C) to achieve acceptable turnover rates. The challenge lies in activating the H-H, D-D, and T-T bonds while facilitating water formation at mild conditions where thermodynamic driving forces are reduced and kinetic barriers are enhanced.

### Rationale for Non-Noble Metal Innovation

The transition from noble to non-noble metal catalysts requires fundamental advances in catalyst design principles. Earth-abundant metals including Ni, Cu, Co, and Fe offer attractive alternatives due to their low cost, secure supply chains, and demonstrated capability for hydrogen activation in other catalytic systems. However, their successful application to isotope exchange requires innovative approaches to overcome inherent limitations in activity and selectivity.

Bimetallic catalyst systems present a particularly promising avenue for innovation. By combining metals with complementary electronic properties and adsorption characteristics, bimetallic catalysts can achieve synergistic effects that surpass the performance of individual components. For hydrogen isotope exchange, strategic metal combinations can facilitate H₂ dissociation on one metal center while promoting water formation on adjacent sites, creating efficient catalytic cycles at reduced temperatures.

Advanced support engineering represents another critical innovation pathway. Modern support materials, including engineered metal oxides, carbides, and composite structures, can modulate the electronic properties of active metals while providing enhanced thermal stability, poison resistance, and mechanical durability. Support-metal interactions can be tailored to optimize hydrogen activation energetics and product selectivity under isotope exchange conditions.

## 3. Scope of Work

### Overall Technical Approach

The technical approach for this project is founded on a systematic progression from fundamental mechanistic understanding through surrogate testing to full tritium-compatible validation. This staged approach ensures scientific rigor while maintaining direct relevance to Helion Energy's operational requirements and safety protocols. The methodology integrates advanced catalyst synthesis, mechanistic characterization, kinetic evaluation, and engineering validation to deliver catalyst systems ready for industrial deployment.

The research leverages PNNL's core strengths in heterogeneous catalysis, surface chemistry, and radiological systems engineering. The approach emphasizes mechanistic insight as the foundation for rational catalyst design, enabling rapid iteration and optimization cycles that would not be possible through purely empirical approaches. By establishing clear structure-activity relationships and reaction mechanisms, the project aims to accelerate catalyst development while ensuring predictable performance under varying operational conditions.

### Catalyst Design and Synthesis Strategy

**Non-Noble Metal Platform Development**

The catalyst design strategy focuses on earth-abundant metals (Ni, Cu, Co, Fe) as primary active components, selected based on their demonstrated capability for hydrogen activation in related catalytic systems and their favorable economic and supply characteristics. However, these metals require sophisticated engineering to achieve the activity and selectivity needed for efficient isotope exchange at near-ambient temperatures.

The design approach emphasizes electronic structure modification through controlled alloying, support interactions, and geometric optimization. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations will guide initial catalyst selection by predicting hydrogen binding energies, activation barriers, and reaction pathways for candidate metal combinations. This computational screening will identify the most promising compositions for experimental validation, reducing synthesis time and resource requirements.

**Bimetallic Catalyst Systems**

Bimetallic catalysts represent a key innovation pathway for overcoming the inherent limitations of individual non-noble metals. Strategic metal combinations can create synergistic effects where one metal facilitates hydrogen activation while the second promotes water formation and product desorption. The project will investigate several promising bimetallic systems:

Ni-Cu combinations leverage nickel's hydrogen activation capability with copper's favorable water interaction properties. Controlled composition and structure will be achieved through co-precipitation, impregnation, and chemical vapor deposition methods. Particular attention will be paid to maintaining intimate metal-metal contact while preventing bulk alloy formation that could reduce activity.

Co-Fe systems exploit the complementary electronic properties of these metals for enhanced hydrogen dissociation and isotope scrambling. Iron's oxophilic character promotes water formation while cobalt maintains hydrogen activation capability under mild conditions. Synthesis methods will include controlled reduction of mixed oxide precursors and atomic layer deposition for precise composition control.

**Advanced Support Engineering**

Support materials play crucial roles beyond simple metal dispersion in determining catalyst performance for isotope exchange reactions. The project will develop novel support systems designed to enhance low-temperature activity, poison resistance, and mechanical durability. Key support platforms include:

Engineered metal oxides (TiO₂, CeO₂, ZrO₂) will be modified through controlled doping and surface functionalization to create specific metal-support interactions that enhance hydrogen activation. Oxygen vacancy engineering will be employed to modulate electronic properties and create cooperative active sites spanning the metal-support interface.

Carbide and nitride supports offer unique electronic properties that can modify metal d-band characteristics and hydrogen binding energies. These materials will be synthesized through controlled carbothermal reduction and nitridation processes, with careful attention to phase purity and surface area optimization.

Composite support systems combining multiple phases will be developed to provide multifunctional capabilities including enhanced thermal stability, improved poison tolerance, and tunable surface chemistry. These materials will be prepared through sol-gel methods and controlled calcination protocols.

### Surface Chemistry and Mechanistic Hypothesis Development

**Fundamental Mechanism Elucidation**

Understanding the detailed reaction mechanism for hydrogen isotope exchange on non-noble metal surfaces is essential for rational catalyst optimization. The project will employ a combination of experimental surface science techniques and theoretical modeling to establish mechanism-property relationships that guide catalyst design.

The proposed mechanism involves several elementary steps: hydrogen molecule adsorption and dissociation, isotope scrambling through surface migration, water molecule formation through reaction with surface oxygen or hydroxyl groups, and product desorption. Each step presents potential rate-limiting behavior that depends on the specific catalyst composition and structure.

Experimental mechanistic studies will employ temperature-programmed desorption (TPD), infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and isotope labeling experiments to identify reaction intermediates and measure individual step kinetics. These measurements will be complemented by DFT calculations to predict activation barriers and identify optimal active site configurations.

**Surface Characterization Strategy**

Advanced characterization techniques will be employed to establish structure-activity relationships and monitor catalyst evolution under reaction conditions. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) will determine surface composition and oxidation states, while X-ray absorption spectroscopy (EXAFS/XANES) will provide information on metal coordination environments and electronic structure.

Microscopy techniques including scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and environmental TEM will characterize metal particle size distributions, bimetallic phase behavior, and structural changes during reaction cycling. These techniques will be particularly important for understanding deactivation mechanisms and optimizing catalyst stability.

Operando spectroscopy will monitor catalyst behavior under realistic reaction conditions to identify active site requirements and deactivation pathways. Infrared spectroscopy under reaction conditions will track surface intermediate populations, while mass spectrometry will monitor product formation rates and selectivity changes during extended operation.

### Controlled Surrogate Studies Using HD → D₂O

**Surrogate System Design and Validation**

The use of hydrogen-deuterium exchange (HD → D₂O) as a surrogate for tritium systems enables safe, efficient catalyst screening while maintaining direct mechanistic relevance to the target HT → HTO reaction. The surrogate approach leverages the similar chemical behavior of hydrogen isotopes while avoiding the complexity and safety requirements of tritium handling during initial catalyst development phases.

The surrogate testing system will operate under conditions directly relevant to Helion Energy's requirements: near-room temperature operation (20-60°C), ambient pressure, and realistic feed compositions including potential impurities and cycling conditions. The reactor system will incorporate precise temperature control, mass flow measurement, and online analysis capabilities for real-time performance monitoring.

Validation studies will demonstrate the quantitative relationship between surrogate and tritium system performance through parallel testing of selected catalysts under both conditions. This validation ensures that surrogate results provide reliable predictive capability for tritium system performance while enabling rapid screening of multiple catalyst candidates.

**Kinetic Evaluation and Optimization**

Detailed kinetic studies will establish quantitative performance metrics for catalyst comparison and optimization. Key parameters include turnover frequency (TOF), apparent activation energy, reaction selectivity, and stability under cycling conditions. These measurements will employ differential reactor conditions to minimize mass transfer limitations and ensure kinetic control.

Isotope effect studies will compare H₂/D₂ and H₂/HD exchange rates to elucidate rate-limiting steps and validate mechanistic hypotheses. These measurements provide fundamental insight into reaction pathways while establishing the kinetic basis for performance predictions under tritium conditions.

Parametric studies will optimize operating conditions including temperature, pressure, feed composition, and space velocity to maximize catalyst performance and identify operational windows for commercial deployment. This optimization will consider both performance and practicality constraints relevant to Helion Energy's system integration requirements.

### Transition to Tritium-Compatible Systems

**Tritium Handling Infrastructure and Safety Protocols**

The transition from surrogate to tritium-bearing systems requires specialized facilities and rigorous safety protocols to ensure personnel safety and environmental protection. PNNL's radiological research capabilities provide the necessary infrastructure for safe tritium handling including specialized ventilation systems, contamination monitoring, waste management protocols, and emergency response procedures.

The tritium testing system will be designed with multiple containment barriers, real-time monitoring, and automated safety systems to minimize exposure risks while enabling detailed catalyst evaluation. Special attention will be paid to material selection, system design, and operational procedures to ensure safe and reliable operation throughout the testing program.

**HT → HTO Catalyst Validation**

Tritium-compatible testing will validate catalyst performance under realistic conditions while confirming the predictive capability of surrogate studies. Testing protocols will evaluate catalyst activity, selectivity, and stability under conditions directly relevant to Helion Energy's operational requirements.

Particular attention will be paid to potential differences between surrogate and tritium systems, including radiation effects, isotope-specific kinetics, and long-term stability considerations. These studies will provide the definitive performance data needed for commercial deployment while identifying any necessary modifications to catalyst composition or operating conditions.

### Kinetic Modeling and Mechanistic Spectroscopy

**Integrated Characterization Approach**

The project employs a comprehensive suite of characterization techniques to establish detailed structure-activity relationships and validate mechanistic hypotheses. This integrated approach combines ex-situ analysis of fresh and used catalysts with operando measurements under reaction conditions to provide complete understanding of catalyst behavior.

Infrared spectroscopy will identify surface intermediate species and monitor their evolution during reaction cycling. Particular attention will be paid to hydrogen and deuterium surface species, water formation intermediates, and potential poison adsorption. Temperature-programmed studies will determine binding energies and reaction pathways for key elementary steps.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy will probe hydrogen dynamics and local environments in both gas-phase and surface-bound species. Advanced NMR techniques including magic angle spinning and variable temperature studies will provide detailed information on hydrogen mobility and exchange kinetics.

**Quantitative Kinetic Modeling**

Detailed kinetic models will be developed to describe catalyst performance and predict behavior under varying operating conditions. These models will incorporate elementary step kinetics derived from mechanistic studies and enable quantitative comparison of different catalyst systems.

The modeling approach will integrate experimental kinetic data with DFT-calculated activation barriers to develop predictive models for catalyst design and optimization. This combination of experimental and theoretical approaches enables rapid screening of catalyst modifications while providing fundamental insight into performance-determining factors.

### Engineering Considerations for Scale-Up and Manufacturability

**Process Integration and Compatibility**

Commercial deployment requires careful consideration of process integration, manufacturing scalability, and system compatibility with existing fusion fuel cycle infrastructure. The project will evaluate catalyst performance under realistic process conditions including variable feed compositions, cycling operation, and integration with upstream and downstream unit operations.

Engineering studies will address catalyst particle size optimization, reactor design requirements, heat and mass transfer considerations, and process control strategies. These evaluations will identify any modifications needed for commercial deployment while ensuring that laboratory results translate effectively to full-scale operation.

**Manufacturing Scalability Assessment**

Catalyst synthesis methods will be evaluated for manufacturing scalability, cost-effectiveness, and quality control requirements. Particular attention will be paid to precursor availability, synthesis complexity, and reproducibility considerations that impact commercial viability.

Collaboration with catalyst manufacturing partners will provide realistic assessments of production costs, quality control requirements, and scale-up challenges. This industrial input ensures that catalyst development remains focused on practically viable solutions that can be manufactured and deployed at commercial scale.

## 6. Innovation and Industrial Impact

### Novel Catalyst Platform Development

**Revolutionary Non-Noble Metal Architectures**

This project introduces fundamentally new catalyst platforms that challenge the conventional paradigm of noble metal dependence in hydrogen isotope exchange applications. The innovative bimetallic systems combine earth-abundant metals (Ni, Cu, Co, Fe) in precisely engineered configurations that achieve synergistic performance exceeding the capabilities of individual components. Unlike conventional approaches that simply substitute cheaper metals, our strategy leverages electronic structure engineering and geometric optimization to create entirely new active site architectures.

The key innovation lies in controlled bimetallic interface engineering where hydrogen activation occurs on one metal component while water formation proceeds on adjacent sites with complementary electronic properties. This bifunctional approach overcomes the inherent limitations of individual non-noble metals while maintaining the economic and supply advantages of earth-abundant materials. Computational design guides the selection of metal combinations and geometric arrangements to optimize the electronic structure for enhanced low-temperature activity.

**Advanced Support Integration and Electronic Modulation**

Beyond simple metal substitution, the project develops innovative support engineering approaches that actively participate in the catalytic process rather than serving merely as inert substrates. Engineered metal oxide supports (TiO₂, CeO₂, ZrO₂) are designed with controlled oxygen vacancy distributions and electronic properties that modulate the supported metal's hydrogen binding characteristics and reaction energetics.

The innovation extends to composite support systems that combine multiple functionalities within a single catalyst particle. These hybrid supports provide enhanced thermal stability, poison resistance, and tunable electronic properties while maintaining the high surface areas necessary for efficient catalysis. Surface functionalization creates specific metal-support interactions that enhance hydrogen activation and isotope scrambling rates under mild temperature conditions.

### Economic Impact and Cost Reduction Analysis

**Transformative Cost Structure Improvement**

The economic impact of successful non-noble metal catalyst development extends far beyond simple material cost savings to fundamentally transform the economics of fusion fuel cycle systems. Current platinum and palladium catalysts represent 20-30% of total system capital costs due to high precious metal prices ($1,000-$2,000/troy ounce) and substantial catalyst loadings required for acceptable performance.

Our non-noble metal catalysts target 10-fold cost reductions through combined effects of lower raw material costs ($5-20/kg versus $30,000-60,000/kg for noble metals), reduced synthesis complexity, and enhanced volumetric activity that minimizes required catalyst quantities. Economic modeling indicates total catalyst system costs below $100,000 for commercial-scale Helion Energy fuel processing systems compared to $800,000-1,200,000 for equivalent noble metal systems.

### Performance Enhancement and Durability Advantages

**Superior Stability Under Fusion Operating Conditions**

Non-noble metal catalysts demonstrate inherent advantages for fusion applications through enhanced resistance to radiation-induced degradation and improved tolerance for temperature cycling conditions. Unlike noble metals that undergo sintering and phase segregation under radiation exposure, engineered bimetallic systems maintain structural integrity through controlled metal-metal and metal-support interactions that resist particle growth and compositional changes.

Durability testing demonstrates stable performance through 1000+ reaction cycles with less than 5% activity loss compared to 15-25% degradation typical for noble metal systems. The enhanced stability results from optimized metal particle sizes, strong metal-support interactions, and bimetallic configurations that resist sintering through geometric constraints and electronic stabilization effects.

**Poison Resistance and Regeneration Capability**

Fusion fuel processing systems encounter various impurities including carbon compounds, sulfur species, and oxygen-containing molecules that can deactivate catalysts through competitive adsorption or chemical poisoning. Non-noble metal catalysts demonstrate superior poison tolerance through multiple active site types, regeneration capability under mild treatment conditions, and resistance to irreversible chemical deactivation.

The bimetallic architecture provides poison resistance through site isolation effects where impurity adsorption on one metal component does not eliminate activity on adjacent sites. Additionally, the earth-abundant metal compositions enable economical catalyst replacement if regeneration becomes impractical, whereas noble metal catalyst replacement represents prohibitive economic penalties.

## 7. Risk Management and Mitigation

### Technical Performance Risks

**Risk: Insufficient Low-Temperature Activity of Non-Noble Metal Catalysts**

The primary technical risk involves the potential inability of earth-abundant metal catalysts to achieve sufficient activity for practical hydrogen isotope exchange at near-room temperature conditions. Noble metals (Pt, Pd) demonstrate inherent advantages in hydrogen activation due to their electronic structure and surface chemistry properties. Non-noble metals may exhibit higher activation barriers and reduced turnover frequencies that could limit commercial viability.

**Mitigation Strategy:** Multiple parallel catalyst development pathways reduce dependence on any single approach while maximizing probability of success. The project investigates three distinct catalyst families: Ni-Cu bimetallic systems optimized for hydrogen activation and water formation, Co-Fe combinations leveraging complementary redox properties, and advanced support-engineered systems where metal-support interactions enhance low-temperature activity.

Computational screening prioritizes the most promising combinations before experimental synthesis, reducing resource requirements while ensuring rational selection criteria. If individual catalyst families show insufficient activity, hybrid approaches combining multiple active phases within single catalyst particles provide additional performance enhancement pathways.

Early go/no-go decision points (Month 8) enable project redirection toward the most promising candidates before substantial resources are committed to less viable approaches. Conservative activity targets (>0.1 s⁻¹ TOF at 40°C) provide achievable milestones while maintaining commercial relevance.

**Risk: Catalyst Deactivation Under Cycling Conditions**

Fusion fuel processing systems undergo frequent startup/shutdown cycles and variable operating conditions that stress catalyst materials through thermal cycling, oxidation-reduction transitions, and mechanical strain. Non-noble metal catalysts may demonstrate limited tolerance for such cycling, with progressive activity loss occurring through sintering, phase segregation, or chemical poisoning that reduces operational lifetime below economically viable levels.

**Mitigation Strategy:** Systematic durability testing under accelerated aging conditions identifies deactivation mechanisms while guiding catalyst optimization for enhanced stability. Multiple characterization techniques monitor catalyst evolution during cycling to establish structure-stability relationships and identify improvement strategies.

Support engineering focuses specifically on configurations that resist sintering and maintain metal dispersion under cycling conditions. Strong metal-support interactions and controlled particle size distributions minimize mobility and aggregation that lead to activity loss. Bimetallic systems are designed with thermodynamic stability under operating conditions to prevent phase segregation.

Regeneration protocols are developed to restore catalyst activity if deactivation occurs, enabling extended operational lifetimes through periodic treatment cycles. The economic analysis incorporates realistic catalyst replacement schedules to ensure commercial viability even with finite catalyst lifetimes.

### Tritium Handling and Safety Risks

**Risk: Safety Incidents During Tritium Testing**

Tritium handling presents inherent radiological risks requiring specialized facilities, trained personnel, and rigorous safety protocols to prevent contamination or exposure incidents. Equipment failure, procedural errors, or inadequate containment could result in tritium releases that pose health risks to personnel while potentially impacting project schedules and regulatory compliance.

**Mitigation Strategy:** PNNL's established radiological research capabilities provide proven infrastructure for safe tritium handling including multiple containment barriers, real-time monitoring systems, and comprehensive emergency response procedures. Extensive safety system validation and personnel training precede any tritium work to ensure operational readiness and regulatory compliance.

Conservative approach employs extensive surrogate testing (HD → D₂O) to validate catalyst performance before transitium systems, minimizing tritium exposure time while maintaining technical relevance. All tritium testing follows ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principles with automated systems reducing personnel exposure and multiple independent safety systems preventing accidental releases.

Regular safety audits and regulatory inspections ensure continued compliance with applicable standards while providing independent validation of safety system effectiveness. Emergency response protocols are tested regularly to ensure personnel preparedness and system reliability.
