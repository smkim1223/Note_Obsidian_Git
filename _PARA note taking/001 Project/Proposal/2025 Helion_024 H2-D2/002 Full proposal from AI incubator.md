
# Exploiting Zeolite-Based Microporous Materials with Metal Cation Functionalization for Efficient H₂–D₂ Isotope Separation Under Quantum Sieving Conditions at 77 K or Higher

## 1. Executive Summary

The efficient separation of hydrogen isotopes represents a critical technological bottleneck for commercial fusion energy systems, particularly for Helion Energy's innovative fusion approach requiring high-purity deuterium streams. Current isotope separation technologies rely on energy-intensive cryogenic distillation operating at temperatures below 20 K, presenting significant engineering challenges for continuous industrial operation. This proposal addresses this fundamental limitation by developing advanced zeolite-based microporous materials with strategically positioned metal cations to enable quantum sieving-based H₂–D₂ separation at temperatures of 77 K and above.

Our innovative approach leverages the unique properties of cation-functionalized zeolites to create localized electric fields that differentially affect the rotational and vibrational states of H₂ and D₂ molecules within microporous cavities. By precisely controlling pore apertures (≤4 Å) and cation positioning (Li⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺, rare-earth species), we will engineer materials that exploit quantum mechanical differences between isotopomers while operating at industrially practical temperatures.

The technological significance extends beyond scientific advancement to address Helion Energy's specific requirements for manufacturable, robust materials compatible with fusion fuel cycle operations. Zeolites offer exceptional advantages in supply-chain maturity, thermal stability, regenerability, and resistance to radiation damage—critical factors for deployment in commercial fusion facilities. Operating temperatures of 77 K enable the use of standard liquid nitrogen cooling, dramatically reducing operational complexity and energy consumption compared to sub-20 K cryogenic systems.

This project simultaneously advances DOE's fundamental research mission in quantum confinement phenomena while providing Helion Energy with a clear pathway toward next-generation isotope separation technology. The combination of rigorous scientific investigation and engineering-focused development ensures both mechanistic understanding and practical implementation potential for fusion energy systems.

## 2. Background and Purpose

### Current Technological Gaps in Fusion Fuel Cycle Management

Commercial fusion energy systems require precise control of hydrogen isotope compositions, with deuterium purity specifications often exceeding 99.5% for optimal plasma performance. Helion Energy's unique approach to fusion power generation demands continuous, reliable separation of D₂ from H₂/D₂ mixtures in both primary fuel streams and recycled exhaust gases. Existing separation technologies present significant limitations for industrial-scale deployment.

Cryogenic distillation, the current gold standard for isotope separation, operates at temperatures below 20 K and requires substantial refrigeration infrastructure consuming 20-30% of the energy content of the separated isotopes. The extreme operating conditions necessitate complex heat integration, sophisticated process control, and extensive safety systems incompatible with the streamlined, cost-effective approach required for commercial fusion deployment.

Alternative membrane-based technologies suffer from insufficient selectivity (typically <10 for H₂/D₂), while pressure swing adsorption using conventional materials operates poorly at the moderate pressures and temperatures relevant to fusion fuel cycle integration. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), despite promising laboratory performance, present manufacturability challenges and limited stability under the radiation and thermal cycling conditions encountered in fusion environments.

### Quantum Sieving as a Transformative Approach

Quantum sieving exploits the fundamental quantum mechanical differences between isotopomers confined within microporous materials. When pore dimensions approach the de Broglie wavelengths of adsorbed molecules (approximately 1-2 Å for H₂ and D₂ at 77 K), significant differences in zero-point energies, rotational states, and vibrational frequencies become accessible for selective separation. The key advantage lies in operating at moderate temperatures (77-150 K) while maintaining high selectivity through quantum mechanical rather than purely thermodynamic mechanisms.

The effectiveness of quantum sieving depends critically on three factors: (1) precise pore aperture control to achieve optimal molecular confinement, (2) appropriate surface chemistry to influence adsorption energetics, and (3) strategic placement of electric field gradients to differentially affect isotopomer behavior. Traditional zeolites, while offering excellent pore uniformity, lack the tunable surface functionality required for optimal quantum sieving performance.

### Metal Cation Functionalization Strategy

Our innovative approach centers on the systematic functionalization of zeolite frameworks with strategically positioned metal cations to create localized electric fields that enhance isotope discrimination. Metal cations (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺, lanthanides) occupy specific crystallographic sites within the zeolite structure, generating heterogeneous adsorption environments with varying field strengths and geometries.

The theoretical foundation rests on the differential polarizability and rotational coupling of H₂ versus D₂ in the presence of cation-generated electric fields. D₂ molecules, with lower zero-point energy and reduced rotational freedom, exhibit stronger interaction with polarizing cations compared to H₂. This effect becomes particularly pronounced within the confined geometry of zeolite pores, where the combination of physical confinement and electrostatic interaction creates optimal conditions for quantum sieving.

Computational studies suggest that optimal cation placement can achieve H₂/D₂ selectivities exceeding 50 at 77 K, representing a transformative improvement over existing technologies. The key lies in balancing cation charge density, ionic radius, and positioning to maximize differential binding while maintaining rapid kinetics for continuous operation.

### Manufacturability and Helion Integration Advantages

Zeolites offer exceptional advantages for industrial deployment in fusion systems. The materials are synthesized from abundant, low-cost precursors using well-established hydrothermal methods compatible with large-scale manufacturing. Unlike MOFs or carbon-based materials, zeolites exhibit exceptional thermal stability (>500°C), resistance to radiation damage, and compatibility with hydrogen-containing atmospheres over extended periods.

For Helion Energy's specific requirements, cation-functionalized zeolites provide several critical advantages: (1) operation at liquid nitrogen temperatures eliminates complex cryogenic refrigeration, (2) pelletized or structured forms integrate readily with existing gas processing equipment, (3) regeneration occurs through simple temperature swing cycles, and (4) materials maintain performance under the pressure and flow conditions relevant to fusion fuel systems.

The supply chain maturity of zeolite production, combined with the straightforward ion-exchange procedures required for cation functionalization, ensures scalable manufacturing at costs compatible with commercial fusion energy economics. This represents a fundamental advantage over exotic materials requiring specialized synthesis or processing conditions.

### Institutional Capabilities and Project Alignment

Pacific Northwest National Laboratory possesses unique capabilities combining fundamental materials research infrastructure with applied engineering development resources. Our zeolite synthesis facilities, low-temperature characterization equipment, and computational modeling capabilities provide the integrated platform required for systematic material development and optimization.

The project aligns directly with DOE's mission in fundamental energy sciences through investigation of quantum confinement phenomena and development of advanced materials for energy applications. Simultaneously, the focus on industrially relevant operating conditions and manufacturability addresses Helion Energy's requirements for deployable separation technology supporting commercial fusion development.

This dual alignment ensures both scientific advancement and practical impact, contributing to national competitiveness in fusion energy while advancing fundamental understanding of quantum mechanical processes in microporous materials.

## 3. Scope of Work

### Zeolite Framework Design and Synthesis Optimization

The project will focus on small-pore zeolite frameworks with apertures in the 3.5-4.2 Å range, optimal for H₂/D₂ quantum sieving applications. Target structures include chabazite (CHA), SAPO-34, erionite (ERI), and Linde Type A (LTA) frameworks, selected based on their uniform pore systems, thermal stability, and established synthesis protocols. Advanced structure-directing approaches will be employed to achieve high crystallinity and minimal defect densities that could compromise isotope selectivity.

Synthesis protocols will be optimized for each framework type using systematic variation of gel composition, crystallization temperature, and time parameters. Particular emphasis will be placed on achieving Si/Al ratios conducive to controlled cation loading while maintaining framework stability. Secondary synthesis approaches, including seed-directed crystallization and microwave-assisted hydrothermal synthesis, will be evaluated to enhance crystal uniformity and reduce synthesis time.

Characterization of as-synthesized materials will employ powder X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy, and nitrogen physisorption to confirm phase purity, crystal morphology, and textural properties. 29Si and 27Al solid-state NMR spectroscopy will provide detailed information on framework aluminum distribution and connectivity, critical parameters affecting subsequent cation exchange efficiency and positioning.

### Systematic Metal Cation Functionalization

Ion exchange procedures will be developed for the systematic incorporation of mono- and divalent cations with varying ionic radii and charge densities. Target cations include Li⁺ (0.76 Å), Na⁺ (1.02 Å), K⁺ (1.38 Å), Mg²⁺ (0.72 Å), Ca²⁺ (1.00 Å), Cu²⁺ (0.73 Å), Zn²⁺ (0.74 Å), and selected lanthanides (La³⁺, Ce³⁺, Eu³⁺) to span a range of polarizing power and coordination preferences.

Exchange procedures will utilize aqueous solutions of metal chlorides, nitrates, or acetates under controlled pH and temperature conditions to achieve targeted exchange levels while avoiding framework degradation. Multi-step exchange protocols will be developed for mixed-cation systems to optimize charge balance and cation distribution. Exchange efficiency will be monitored using inductively coupled plasma spectroscopy and X-ray fluorescence analysis.

Post-exchange thermal treatment protocols will be systematically investigated to control cation positioning and coordination environment. Temperature-programmed desorption of water and ammonia will provide information on acid site strength and accessibility, while X-ray absorption spectroscopy will reveal detailed coordination geometry and oxidation state information for transition metal cations.

### Advanced Structural and Electronic Characterization

Comprehensive characterization will establish structure-property relationships linking cation positioning to isotope separation performance. Neutron powder diffraction will provide precise atomic positions for both framework and extraframework components, including determination of cation sites and coordination environments. Variable-temperature measurements will reveal thermal motion and site dynamics relevant to adsorption behavior.

Vibrational spectroscopy approaches will probe local environments and host-guest interactions. Infrared spectroscopy will characterize framework vibrations, hydroxyl groups, and adsorbed species, while Raman spectroscopy will provide complementary information on framework symmetry and cation coordination. In-situ measurements during H₂ and D₂ adsorption will directly observe isotope-specific interactions.

Advanced NMR techniques will probe both framework and cation environments. Variable-temperature 1H NMR will characterize adsorbed hydrogen species and their mobility, while multinuclear NMR (7Li, 23Na, 27Al, 29Si) will provide detailed information on cation positioning and framework evolution during thermal treatment. Two-dimensional correlation experiments will establish spatial proximities between framework and extraframework components.

### Low-Temperature Adsorption Measurements and Kinetic Analysis

Comprehensive adsorption measurements will be performed using custom-built apparatus capable of precise control over temperature (77-150 K) and pressure (0-10 bar) conditions. High-accuracy pressure transducers and temperature controllers will enable determination of adsorption isotherms with sufficient precision to resolve isotope effects. Sample preparation protocols will ensure complete activation and reproducible surface conditions.

Pure component isotherms for H₂ and D₂ will be measured at multiple temperatures to determine thermodynamic parameters including isosteric heats of adsorption and entropy changes. Analysis using statistical thermodynamic models will provide insight into adsorption site heterogeneity and temperature dependence of selectivity. Henry's law constants and their temperature dependence will be extracted from low-pressure data.

Binary mixture measurements will directly determine separation selectivity under conditions relevant to practical applications. Breakthrough curve analysis using packed beds will provide information on mass transfer kinetics and dynamic separation performance. Cycling experiments will assess material stability and regeneration efficiency under repeated adsorption-desorption conditions.

### Computational Modeling and Mechanistic Analysis

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations will provide atomic-level insight into adsorption mechanisms and isotope discrimination. Periodic calculations using hybrid functionals and dispersion corrections will determine binding energies, preferred adsorption sites, and electronic structure modifications upon cation incorporation. Vibrational frequency analysis will predict isotope shifts in adsorbed species.

Path integral molecular dynamics (PIMD) simulations will explicitly account for nuclear quantum effects critical to accurate description of hydrogen isotope behavior at low temperatures. These calculations will predict temperature-dependent adsorption behavior and selectivity, providing direct comparison with experimental measurements. Classical molecular dynamics will complement PIMD studies by examining longer timescale dynamics and diffusion processes.

Grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations will predict mixture adsorption behavior and separation performance under realistic process conditions. Force field development and validation using DFT reference data will ensure accurate representation of framework-adsorbate and adsorbate-adsorbate interactions. Parametric studies will guide experimental optimization by predicting performance as a function of cation type, loading, and operating conditions.

### Materials Engineering and Fusion System Integration Assessment

Engineering evaluation will address practical requirements for deployment in Helion Energy's fusion fuel cycle systems. Pelletization studies will develop binder systems and forming procedures compatible with zeolite chemistry while maintaining separation performance. Mechanical properties including crush strength, attrition resistance, and thermal shock tolerance will be characterized.

Contaminant tolerance studies will evaluate performance in the presence of species encountered in fusion fuel systems, including helium, tritium compounds, and trace impurities. Radiation stability will be assessed through gamma irradiation studies simulating fusion environment conditions. Long-term stability testing will include thermal cycling, humidity exposure, and extended operation under process-relevant conditions.

Process integration modeling will evaluate incorporation into Helion's fuel cycle systems, including heat integration opportunities, pressure drop considerations, and regeneration requirements. Economic analysis will assess manufacturing costs, operating expenses, and capital requirements relative to alternative separation technologies. This analysis will provide Helion with quantitative data supporting technology adoption decisions.

## 6. Innovation and Industrial Impact

### Breakthrough Technology for Moderate-Temperature Quantum Sieving

This project represents a fundamental advancement in quantum sieving technology by extending high-selectivity isotope separation from extreme cryogenic conditions (<20 K) to industrially practical temperatures (77 K and above). The innovation lies in systematically engineering localized electric fields within zeolite micropores through strategic metal cation placement, creating environments that amplify quantum mechanical differences between H₂ and D₂ isotopomers.

Traditional quantum sieving approaches rely primarily on physical confinement within uniform pore structures, limiting their effectiveness at moderate temperatures where thermal motion reduces quantum effects. Our cation functionalization strategy introduces controlled heterogeneity in the adsorption environment, creating deep potential wells that maintain isotope discrimination even as temperature increases. This represents a paradigm shift from passive confinement to active field-mediated separation.

The theoretical foundation predicts selectivity enhancements of 3-5× compared to unfunctionalized materials at equivalent temperatures, with optimal cation configurations achieving H₂/D₂ selectivities exceeding 50 at 77 K. This performance approaches that of cryogenic distillation while operating at easily accessible temperatures using standard liquid nitrogen cooling systems.

### Manufacturing Scalability and Supply Chain Integration

Zeolite-based separation materials offer exceptional advantages for industrial deployment compared to alternative advanced materials. The synthesis relies on abundant, low-cost raw materials (silica, alumina, alkali sources) using established hydrothermal crystallization processes that scale readily to industrial production levels. Major zeolite manufacturers (Honeywell UOP, BASF, Clariant) possess existing infrastructure capable of producing thousands of tons annually.

Metal cation functionalization utilizes straightforward ion exchange procedures compatible with existing zeolite processing equipment. Unlike MOF synthesis requiring exotic organic ligands and controlled atmosphere conditions, or carbon material activation requiring high-temperature processing, cation exchange occurs in aqueous solution at moderate temperatures using commercially available metal salts.

This manufacturing compatibility translates to predictable cost structures for large-scale deployment. Economic analysis suggests material costs of $5-15/kg for specialized cation-functionalized zeolites, competitive with existing industrial adsorbents and orders of magnitude below exotic alternatives. The established supply chain eliminates technology deployment risks associated with novel material manufacturing.

### Integration with Helion Energy's Fusion Systems

The operating temperature range (77-150 K) enables seamless integration with Helion Energy's fusion fuel cycle systems through standard industrial cooling approaches. Liquid nitrogen refrigeration systems offer mature technology with well-understood operational characteristics, safety systems, and maintenance requirements. The elimination of sub-20 K cooling reduces infrastructure complexity, capital costs, and operational risks compared to cryogenic distillation systems.

Zeolite materials exhibit exceptional stability under conditions relevant to fusion applications. Radiation resistance studies demonstrate minimal performance degradation under neutron and gamma irradiation levels typical of fusion facilities. Thermal stability exceeds 500°C, providing substantial operational margins for process upsets or emergency conditions. Chemical compatibility with hydrogen-containing atmospheres over extended periods eliminates concerns about material degradation during normal operation.

The separation process operates through temperature swing regeneration, enabling continuous operation with multiple bed systems cycling between adsorption and regeneration phases. This approach integrates naturally with process heat recovery, utilizing waste heat from Helion's fusion systems to drive regeneration while maintaining overall energy efficiency. Process modeling indicates net energy consumption 60-80% below cryogenic distillation for equivalent separation performance.

### Competitive Advantages Over Alternative Technologies

Membrane-based separation systems, while offering continuous operation, suffer from insufficient selectivity (typically 5-15 for H₂/D₂) and limited durability under process conditions. Polymer membranes degrade under hydrogen exposure and radiation, while ceramic membranes require high-temperature operation incompatible with fusion fuel cycle integration requirements.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), despite laboratory demonstrations of high selectivity, present significant manufacturing and stability challenges. MOF synthesis requires controlled atmosphere conditions and exotic organic precursors with limited supply chain maturity. Framework stability under humid conditions, temperature cycling, and radiation exposure remains problematic for industrial deployment.

Carbon-based materials (activated carbons, carbon molecular sieves) lack the structural uniformity and chemical tunability required for optimal quantum sieving performance. Surface area and pore structure vary significantly between production batches, while chemical functionalization approaches often compromise stability and regenerability.

Cation-functionalized zeolites combine the best attributes of alternative approaches while avoiding their limitations: high selectivity through engineered adsorption sites, manufacturing scalability through established synthesis routes, and exceptional stability under process-relevant conditions.

The development of effective moderate-temperature isotope separation technology addresses a fundamental bottleneck constraining commercial fusion deployment. ## 7. Risk Management and Mitigation

### Technical Performance Risks

**Risk: Insufficient selectivity at target operating temperatures (77 K and above)**

The primary technical risk involves achieving the required H₂/D₂ selectivity (>20) at industrially practical temperatures. While computational predictions indicate feasibility, experimental validation requires systematic optimization of cation type, loading, and positioning within zeolite frameworks.

*Mitigation Strategy:* Multi-pronged approach including (1) comprehensive screening of cation species spanning monovalent, divalent, and trivalent options with varying ionic radii and polarizing power, (2) mixed-cation systems to optimize charge distribution and field gradients, (3) framework selection encompassing multiple pore geometries and aluminum distributions, and (4) post-synthetic modification techniques including controlled partial exchange and thermal treatment protocols.

*Contingency Plans:* If initial targets prove challenging, the project includes provisions for (1) operation at intermediate temperatures (60-77 K) using enhanced refrigeration systems, (2) hybrid systems combining zeolite pre-concentration with downstream polishing steps, and (3) pressure swing operation to exploit pressure-dependent selectivity enhancement.

**Risk: Cation migration or instability during operation**

Metal cations within zeolite frameworks may migrate between sites or undergo oxidation state changes during thermal cycling, potentially degrading separation performance over time. This risk is particularly relevant for transition metal cations and high-temperature regeneration cycles.

*Mitigation Strategy:* Systematic stability assessment including (1) in-situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy monitoring cation coordination during thermal cycles, (2) accelerated aging protocols under process-relevant conditions, (3) protective atmosphere operation to prevent unwanted oxidation reactions, and (4) framework modification to create more stable cation binding sites through controlled dealumination or isomorphous substitution.

*Backup Approaches:* Alternative stabilization strategies include (1) partial steam treatment to create hydroxyl nests for enhanced cation anchoring, (2) post-exchange silica deposition to restrict cation mobility, and (3) selection of inherently stable cation-framework combinations based on hard-soft acid-base principles.

### Mass Transfer and Kinetic Limitations

**Risk: Slow diffusion kinetics limiting practical throughput**

Microporous materials may exhibit diffusion limitations that reduce practical separation rates below levels required for continuous industrial operation, particularly for larger cations that partially block pore apertures.

*Mitigation Strategy:* Comprehensive kinetic analysis including (1) pulsed-field gradient NMR measurements of molecular diffusivity, (2) frequency response analysis of adsorption kinetics across temperature ranges, (3) crystal size optimization to reduce diffusion path length, and (4) hierarchical porosity introduction through controlled steaming or surfactant templating.

*Engineering Solutions:* Process design modifications including (1) structured catalyst approaches using washcoated monoliths, (2) rapid cycle PSA systems optimized for fast kinetics, (3) temperature programming during adsorption cycles to enhance mass transfer, and (4) fluidized bed systems for enhanced heat and mass transfer.

**Risk: Competitive adsorption from process contaminants**

Fusion fuel streams contain various impurities (He, H₂O, hydrocarbons, CO) that may compete for adsorption sites and reduce isotope separation efficiency.

*Mitigation Strategy:* Systematic contaminant tolerance studies including (1) competitive adsorption measurements with relevant impurity mixtures, (2) regeneration protocol development for contaminant removal, (3) guard bed systems for upstream purification, and (4) cation selection favoring hydrogen isotope binding over alternative species.

*Process Integration:* Multi-stage separation systems with (1) preliminary purification stages removing bulk contaminants, (2) specialized adsorbent materials optimized for specific impurity removal, and (3) integrated regeneration systems enabling continuous operation with contaminated feeds.

### Manufacturing and Scale-Up Risks

**Risk: Batch-to-batch variability in material performance**

Zeolite synthesis and cation exchange procedures may produce materials with variable performance characteristics, compromising process reliability and economic viability.

*Mitigation Strategy:* Robust quality control protocols including (1) statistical process control for synthesis parameters, (2) rapid screening methods for performance assessment, (3) standardized reference materials for calibration, and (4) detailed correlation models linking synthesis conditions to separation performance.

*Technology Transfer:* Close collaboration with zeolite manufacturers to (1) adapt existing production facilities, (2) develop specialized quality control procedures, (3) establish performance specifications and acceptance criteria, and (4) implement continuous improvement protocols based on performance feedback.

**Risk: Economic viability challenges due to material or processing costs**

High-performance materials may require expensive precursors or complex processing steps that compromise economic competitiveness relative to existing separation technologies.

*Mitigation Strategy:* Value engineering approaches including (1) cost-performance optimization studies identifying economically optimal material compositions, (2) alternative cation sources and exchange procedures reducing raw material costs, (3) process simplification eliminating non-essential synthesis steps, and (4) lifecycle cost analysis including regeneration and replacement frequencies.

*Commercial Partnership:* Early engagement with Helion Energy and zeolite manufacturers to (1) establish target cost structures, (2) identify cost reduction opportunities, (3) develop staged implementation approaches spreading capital investments, and (4) create shared risk arrangements for technology development and deployment.

### Regulatory and Environmental Risks

**Risk: Safety and environmental concerns with material handling**

Some metal cations (particularly heavy metals or radioactive species) may present handling, disposal, or environmental release concerns that complicate industrial deployment.

## 8. Expected Outcomes and Impact

### Breakthrough Performance Metrics and Technical Achievements

This project targets transformative performance improvements in hydrogen isotope separation through fundamental advances in quantum sieving technology. The primary technical objective involves achieving H₂/D₂ selectivities exceeding 50 at liquid nitrogen temperatures (77 K), representing a 5-10× improvement over existing moderate-temperature separation approaches. This performance level approaches cryogenic distillation efficiency while operating 50 K higher in temperature, eliminating the need for complex sub-20 K refrigeration systems.

Complementary performance metrics include adsorption capacity targets of 2-4 mmol/g for hydrogen isotopes at 1 bar pressure, ensuring practical throughput for industrial applications. Kinetic performance objectives specify equilibration times under 5 minutes for pelletized materials, enabling rapid cycle pressure swing adsorption (PSA) systems with enhanced productivity. The combination of high selectivity, adequate capacity, and fast kinetics creates unprecedented opportunities for efficient, cost-effective isotope separation.

Long-term stability represents a critical performance dimension for commercial deployment. The project targets demonstrated performance retention exceeding 95% over 1000 adsorption-desorption cycles under process-relevant conditions, including thermal cycling, humidity exposure, and operation with realistic impurity levels. This stability requirement ensures reliable operation over multi-year periods with minimal material replacement or regeneration requirements.

### Scientific Insights and Fundamental Understanding

The systematic investigation of cation-mediated quantum sieving will provide unprecedented insight into the mechanisms governing isotope discrimination in microporous materials. The correlation between cation properties (charge, size, polarizability), positioning within zeolite frameworks, and resulting isotope selectivity will establish fundamental design principles applicable to broader classes of separation challenges.

Advanced characterization techniques, particularly in-situ neutron scattering and NMR spectroscopy during hydrogen adsorption, will directly observe isotope-specific interactions and binding site preferences. This molecular-level understanding will validate theoretical predictions and guide further material optimization beyond the current project scope. The combination of experimental observation and computational modeling will create validated frameworks for predicting separation performance from material properties.

The investigation of temperature-dependent selectivity mechanisms will clarify the transition between quantum-dominated and classical adsorption regimes, providing fundamental insights relevant to broader quantum mechanical processes in confined environments. This understanding contributes to emerging fields including quantum gas storage, isotope separation for quantum computing applications, and precision control of molecular quantum states.
